PRODUCT ENGINEERING / MY OWN PROJECTS

PathMove, PathOps and PathAssure

These projects bring together the customer journey, the work staff need to do and my idea for following an operation from preparation through daily use.

I am building PathMove as the customer-facing experience and PathOps as the staff operations layer. PathAssure is a separate concept for operational readiness and assurance. They address different problems, even where the ideas about evidence, permissions and handover connect.

Current developmentPathMove is a working demo with fictional, device-local records. PathOps combines a synthetic staff console with implemented release-preparation and public-content functions. The console’s role switching is a demo, not live authentication. PathAssure is a concept with mockups, not an implemented platform.

01 / SEPARATING THE JOBS

The customer and the reviewer need different things.

A customer needs to understand the next step in their own journey. A member of staff needs to understand the work assigned to them, the information behind it and which actions they are allowed to take. A release needs to identify exactly which version was approved.

I am keeping those responsibilities separate. PathMove presents the customer journey and customer-safe content. PathOps holds the staff workflows and release controls. The content contract defines what can cross between them.

This matters because an attractive interface does not decide who has authority. A button can show an action, but the backend still needs to establish who is making the request, which product and environment it applies to, and whether the action is permitted.

PathAssure looks at a different point in the work: whether an operation is ready to launch and whether the evidence still supports confidence in it afterwards. I have developed that product structure and the proposed lifecycle, while keeping it clear that the platform remains to be built.

02 / PATHMOVE

Ask what the person wants to do first.

I built the frontend in React and TypeScript with Vite. The journey starts with the person’s objective rather than expecting them to know a visa name or understand the professional terminology before they can begin.

That first decision leads to the relevant questions and preparation work. The idea is to make the intake follow the task, rather than put every possible question in front of every person.

Different workspaces for different users

The frontend includes journeys for individuals, recruiters, employers and agencies. These roles do not all approach the work in the same way. An individual is following their own preparation, while a professional workspace needs to organize information and handover around a different responsibility.

I have modelled preparation state, next actions, pathway guidance and checklists in the demo. Fictional records let me work through those interactions without collecting real customer documents or creating a payment.

Preparation and handover

The customer experience includes more than the initial questions. I have worked on the preparation steps and how the information is organized for review and professional handover. The interface needs to show what has been done, what remains and what someone should do next.

I also separate the summaries for entry, stay and work in the content contract. Those are different subjects, and combining them into one vague permission statement would make the guidance harder to interpret. The portfolio shows how the interface handles the information; it does not make an immigration decision.

Developing without live customer activity

The current demo uses fictional, device-local records and a same-origin sample-content fixture. Payments are disabled and document handling is visual-only. This gives me a way to develop the user journey while keeping live service activation separate from the interface work.

The homepage and intake screenshots are actual captures from that local demo. They show the state of the frontend and the sample journey I was testing.

03 / THE CONTENT CONTRACT

The frontend checks what it receives.

I built a typed adapter between the customer interface and the content it reads. The adapter carries the product, environment and contract version, then parses the response before the interface uses it.

The public page shape includes a title, summary, body and page type. It also carries a canonical path and indexability state, review information, source information and separate entry, stay and work summaries.

Content needs its review context.

The review fields include status, review time, an effective date where applicable and the reviewer role. The source records distinguish official and supporting sources and can carry an observation time and limitations.

The release information identifies the release and content version, the version digest, activation time, freshness and the last check. That lets the customer layer receive more than an unlabelled block of text.

Reject an unexpected response.

The parser checks the contract and context, field shapes, identifiers, digest format and release state. It also checks the source and path information. Unknown fields and incompatible context are not silently accepted as if they were part of the agreed response.

I have separate failure states for offline, unavailable, unsupported version, forbidden, conflict, retryable failure and invalid response. Freshness can also be current or stale. Those distinctions let the interface show the relevant condition instead of treating every problem as the same generic error.

The current demo transport reads a synthetic fixture. The typed contract and parser are implemented, while a live service connection remains a separate step.

04 / PATHOPS

The staff tools need to show the work and its limits.

I built the React staff console around role-specific workspaces, queues, reviews, approvals and audit views. The demonstration uses fictional records so I can examine the workflow and the information staff would need at each step.

The shared shell uses a narrow staff-area rail with a contextual operations menu. A selected module expands the links for that area. I wanted one navigation system that stayed understandable as the workspace changed, rather than another menu being added inside every page.

Role-specific work

The demonstration covers staff functions such as platform administration, content preparation, qualified review, release operation, customer service, finance, privacy and marketing/growth. The current role picker also includes more specific departmental and manager views.

Switching a fictional role changes the navigation and available demo actions. That helps me work through the interface. It does not establish an authenticated identity or replace server-side authorization.

Keep review, approval and release separate.

A person preparing content is not automatically the person qualified to approve it, and approval is not automatically publication. The console separates those stages so the assigned work and the decision can be examined.

The review workspace includes source evidence and baseline-versus-shadow comparison. The approval workspace shows the selected version and the actions available to the demo role. Disabled actions are part of showing the intended limit rather than letting every role do everything.

Marketing and growth is also an operations workspace.

I developed a command centre with a priority queue, an in-place detail drawer and an objectives register. The wider workspace includes planning, campaign records, opportunities, page work, search and AI-visibility observations, social assets, partnerships, lifecycle/CRM views, performance and experiments.

Those areas use synthetic records. The interface can show the proposed work, its evidence and governance, but it does not launch campaigns, send social posts, synchronize audiences, make payments or contact customers.

The point of that work is to make the operational responsibility visible: which record is being reviewed, what information supports it and which action still needs a person with the right authority.

05 / THE IMPLEMENTED BACKEND WORK

Prepare the approved version, with the right authority.

I implemented backend functions for content-release preparation and reading public-safe content. The release-preparation operation requires an already-approved version whose SHA-256 digest matches the content being prepared.

That function prepares a release record. Publishing or cutting over a production service is a separate action. I have kept the first backend operation small enough to examine the permission checks, approval relationship, audit and retry behaviour together.

Identity comes from the request context.

The callable entry point requires App Check. The actor comes from the authenticated request context, and active grants are resolved on the server. A role written into the request body is not treated as authority.

The permission checks bind the action to the product and environment. A grant for one product or environment should not become permission to perform the same action somewhere else.

Validate the request before changing records.

The request validation is closed. It checks the permitted fields, identifiers, environment, digest, reason and exact-version relationships, and rejects unknown fields. That makes the request shape part of the operation rather than allowing extra values to be passed through without a decision.

The approval names a particular content digest. If the content changes, the earlier approval cannot simply be reused for a different version.

The release, audit and retry receipt belong together.

I used a Firestore transaction to create the prepared release, audit record and idempotency receipt together. The operation should not leave a release recorded without the related audit intent.

If an identical request is retried with the same identity, it can return the stored result. If someone reuses the same key with changed input, the operation returns a conflict. This is the difference between safely repeating the original operation and accidentally preparing another release.

Keep protected records out of direct client access.

The protected backend data has deny-all client rules. Customer content is returned as a versioned public-safe projection, rather than giving the frontend access to staff collections.

Failures use stable error codes and a correlation identifier. That supports diagnosis without returning an internal exception to the client.

The release-preparation sequence
Authenticated request and App Check
Check product, environment, action and exact version
Resolve active grants on the server
Check the approval and content digest
Transaction: prepared release, audit record and retry receipt
Return the prepared result or a stable error

06 / PATHASSURE

Are we ready, are we under control, and what may go wrong next?

PathAssure is my concept for operational readiness and assurance. I developed it around the gap between having separate checklists, monitoring screens, project plans and documents, and being able to make a supported decision about the operation.

The proposed lifecycle is prepare, validate, go live, operate, monitor, predict and improve. The same record should remain useful after launch, rather than losing the readiness work when the project is handed over.

Before go-live

In readiness mode, I want to bring together the infrastructure, applications, integrations, vendors, people, training, documentation, testing and dependencies. Each item needs an owner and supporting information, not only a green status.

The proposed decision states include go, go with conditions, blocked, insufficient evidence and management review required. A missing critical condition should remain visible even when other parts of the project are complete.

After go-live

In assurance mode, the same operational record would retain the readiness history and add controls, telemetry, incidents, maintenance, evidence renewal and vendor performance. The question changes from whether we can launch to whether we can show that the operation remains under control.

This could be useful for a hotel opening, a new production line, an IT go-live, a site expansion or a management handover. These are proposed use cases, not completed PathAssure customer deployments.

The historical mockup uses the former AssuranceOS name. Sites, values, forecasts and certification indicators are examples.

07 / THE PROPOSED ASSURANCE MODEL

Keep the evidence behind the status.

The concept separates the control, the evidence and the risk. A control describes what needs to work and how it should be checked. Evidence records what was actually checked, when it was collected, who owns it and when it needs renewal. A risk can follow from a failed control, missing evidence, an incident or an unresolved dependency.

A backup example

A backup being configured is one question. Whether the latest job worked is another. Whether a restore has been tested, who owns recovery and whether the recovery objectives are defined are further questions. I want those to remain connected rather than collapse into one unchecked “backup complete” field.

The proposed evidence record retains its source, owner, collection time, expiry, classification, related control, verification result and history. That would make it possible to see both the result and how it was reached.

Readiness, assurance and confidence are different questions.

The proposed readiness measure asks whether the operation can proceed or launch. Assurance asks whether the live operation can show its controls are working. Operational confidence asks what the information and trends suggest about future stability.

I have not approved a scoring formula or trained a forecasting model. A percentage in the mockup is an illustration. A useful confidence measure would need a forecast horizon, contributing factors, data-quality information, an explanation and a clear distinction between observed facts, rules and statistical inference.

A high score must not hide a blocker.

The design calls for critical conditions to remain visible. A high total should not override a security, recovery, safety or other go-live-critical issue. Manual overrides would need a reason and history.

AI should help explain and prioritize information. The accountable decision still needs to remain with a person. I would start with explainable controls and rules before trying to use predictive models for operational decisions.

Start with a defined part of the problem.

The proposed first version centres on readiness records, owners, evidence, actions, review and the transition into ongoing assurance. Integrations, multi-site views and forecasting would need to be developed and tested around that foundation. The product remains a concept with a dossier and interface direction.

08 / TESTING AND NEXT WORK

I want to know what the implementation actually does.

The PathOps local tests cover successful release preparation, missing or inactive identity, product/environment denial, invalid requests, approval mismatch, repeated requests and changed input using the same retry key. Emulator records also cover direct-access denial and the transaction that creates the release, audit and receipt.

The PathMove adapter has contract and parsing checks around the permitted public response. I also captured the local interfaces to check that the demo states and navigation were understandable in the browser.

Those are local implementation and demo checks. Live authentication, production acceptance and any future service activation need their own verification. I keep that separate from what can already be seen in the code and screenshots.

For PathAssure, the next work is different: validate the problem and the first use case, define the controls and evidence model, and build a small working implementation. A detailed concept is useful preparation, but it is not a released platform.
