NEW WAVE GROUP / 2023 TO 2025 ROLE

The Microsoft 365 migration

This was one of my most challenging projects and one of the projects I am most proud of. The work was not only moving files. It was understanding the data, the people, the old infrastructure and the business deadlines, then making a plan the team could carry out.

I led the 12-person project team for the move from on-premise Active Directory to Microsoft 365 and Entra ID, including OneDrive. I worked closely with the PM on the technical pre-study, priorities, execution and documentation.

The wider New Wave environment had more than 5,000 users. Central IT was based in Sweden, Belgium and the Netherlands, with local IT contacts at the individual companies and sites. The migration had to work through that organization, not only through the technology.

01 / MY RESPONSIBILITY

I worked with the PM to fill the technical gaps.

The PM coordinated the project, and I worked alongside that coordination with the technical research and planning. I worked on the tools, processes and file structures for moving business-critical files from legacy servers into Microsoft 365, including SharePoint. My responsibility continued into preparation, execution, troubleshooting and helping users with the change. We needed to understand what data was involved, which tools and approach to use, where to start and which companies or users needed particular attention.

That work connected the central plan with what was actually happening in the companies. The project could look straightforward from a list of servers while the data and the way people worked were much more complicated.

I also had other responsibilities at New Wave. MacOps, the KACE-to-Intune migration, backups, printing and Exchange were separate workstreams with different teams. I worked through the project teams and their coordinators.

02 / UNDERSTANDING THE WORK

The servers did not tell the whole story.

The project turned out to be larger than expected. The original view did not fully show how much data departments such as finance held or how much information people were using in their daily work.

People also had different arrangements. Some work was on the computers or on-premise storage. Some people already used OneDrive or another cloud-storage arrangement. We needed to understand those differences before deciding how to move the work.

Ask the technical questions before execution.

I worked on the pre-study with the PM so we could choose the tools, the method and the order. That included deciding where to begin and which users, roles or companies were most important to handle carefully at a particular stage.

My main MacOps responsibility also gave me a view of the creative users and how they worked with their files. The migration needed to account for that working environment rather than assume every user’s storage and deadlines were the same.

For me, the pre-study was not a document to finish and forget. It was how we found the missing information and turned it into a practical execution plan.

03 / BUSINESS DEADLINES AND PEOPLE

Finance had reporting dates. Creative teams had catalogue deadlines.

I raised those dates in the planning meetings. A migration sequence needed to reflect the work the departments were trying to deliver, not only the order in the technical schedule.

That meant working with the PM and the local contacts to understand who could move when. A company or department could be technically ready while the timing still made the change difficult for the people doing the work.

Explain what the file transfer requires.

During the relevant transfer stage, people needed to understand when they could not work on or change the source files. Some accepted that easily. Others wanted to continue working and had much less patience with the restriction.

I had to explain it while also understanding why they were frustrated. They had their own deadlines, and IT was asking them to stop or change something about how they worked. That part of the project took patience and communication, not only technical knowledge.

Work with the local organization.

The local IT contacts were important because they understood their own company and site. Central IT could provide the shared infrastructure and direction, but we still needed that local knowledge to make the change workable.

I could not rely on direct authority over every person involved. I needed to make the technical requirements clear, listen to the local situation and work through the plan with the PM and contacts.

04 / WHEN THE PLAN HAD TO CHANGE

Server health could become more urgent than the schedule.

The migration was connected to the lifecycle of the on-premise servers. We needed to consider support dates and the health of the equipment, not only the date a company had been assigned in the plan.

One company was scheduled months ahead, but a storage failure meant the work could not simply wait for that slot. We had to change the priority and decide what needed to happen immediately.

I worked with the PM on that change: what to do, how to do it and how it affected the rest of the work. The plan needed to respond to the condition of the infrastructure.

Keep the whole change in view.

Moving the data was connected to what happened to the old equipment afterwards. We needed to understand the remaining dependencies and what would happen when the on-premise servers were shut down.

That was part of why I liked the project. I could work with the technical details while also seeing how the decision affected the users, the other teams and the later work.

05 / EXECUTION THROUGH A TEAM

I made the plan so other people could do it.

I could not perform every migration myself. The project needed a method the whole team could use and continue. I documented how to carry out the work and helped turn the technical decisions into a plan others could follow.

That was different from knowing how to solve one migration on my own. The process needed to be understandable to colleagues who would be doing the next part without me standing beside them.

I worked with PMO and the team coordination to connect the planned work with execution. When information was missing or a dependency changed, I needed to bring that into the discussion rather than let the team continue with an assumption that no longer held.

The same concern runs through my other infrastructure work: the organization should not become dependent on one person remembering how everything works.

06 / HANDOVER AND WHAT I LEARNED

Bring technical people into the project earlier.

One of my biggest lessons was that the PM and management do not know everything from the start. Technical staff need to help discover the scope early, especially where the data, working methods and old equipment differ between companies.

I learned a great deal about how people respond to a change, how much patience that can require and how important it is to understand the work behind their deadlines.

I was also proud of the documentation. Colleagues could continue using the method, and the plan included the later lifecycle of the old servers. I wanted the work to be useful beyond the migration I happened to be doing that day.

This is why I describe the project as both challenging and rewarding. It brought together infrastructure, the business, project coordination and the practical responsibility of getting the work carried out by a team.
