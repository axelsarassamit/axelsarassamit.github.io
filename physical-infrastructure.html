PHYSICAL INFRASTRUCTURE / WORKING METHODS

Build it so the next person can work on it.

Organized cables, usable records and a clear handover are part of the engineering. They help turn an installation into something people can maintain.

Hands-on scopeMy work includes customer rack and network installations at LOBIC and physical infrastructure support at New Wave Group. The examples below come from my own interview accounts; the installation photographs show separate examples of my work.

01 / CABLING

Make a fault easier to find.

I organize cable routes with future access in mind. A tidy rack is useful when someone needs to trace a connection, remove a faulty lead or replace equipment under time pressure.

Test and retain the result

In the interviews, I emphasized testing cables and keeping the results with the installation documentation. A connected cable and an assurance that it was tested are different from a record that the next engineer can check.

Labels that connect to a record

I use a cable identifier that can be followed into the documentation, alongside clear equipment and location labels. The purpose is to make the connection traceable without relying on the memory of the person who installed it.

A cable comb adapted to my work

I redesigned a cable comb and had a friend 3D-print it. The design introduced a twist in the outer cable arrangement while keeping individual cables accessible. This was a practical tool for how I wanted to organize the bundle.

I have not published a controlled electrical-performance test of that design, so I do not claim a measured reduction in crosstalk or an industry-certified improvement.

02 / RECORDS

Capture in Excel, reconcile in NetBox.

I described using Excel while working on site to record cable identifiers, switch names and port connections. Capturing the information in a simple table let me keep up with the physical work, then enter or import the records into NetBox afterwards.

My interview account places this work at New Wave. I avoid claiming that every cable across the entire group was inventoried, since the transcript alone does not verify complete coverage.

03 / OPERATIONS

Keep the room, equipment and procedures connected.

At New Wave, my responsibilities included hands-on support in small server-room environments, equipment changes, backup operations and UPS work. This is enterprise on-site infrastructure experience, not a claim of delivering a hyperscale or GPU data centre.

Learn from a cooling incident

I described responding to an air-conditioning failure and then revising emergency cooling procedures and adding room monitoring. The lesson was to make the response easier to follow before another incident, rather than leaving it dependent on whoever happened to be present.

The account does not establish a tested thermal model or a measured no-downtime result. Detailed room layouts, equipment placement and emergency operating instructions remain private.

Documentation is part of the handover

My aim is for a colleague to continue the work if I am unavailable. That means keeping labels, records and procedures understandable, and sharing the method with the team instead of retaining all the knowledge myself.

04 / SUPPLIER COORDINATION

Find the next action and get the commitment in writing.

In scenario questions about contractor errors and missing deliveries, I described an approach based on clear communication: identify the problem, agree the corrective action, consider a temporary solution where appropriate, and obtain a written delivery commitment.

That is my stated working method. It is not presented as a completed contractor case with an invented defect rate, recovery time or commercial outcome.
